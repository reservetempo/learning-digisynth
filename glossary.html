Glossary

Every alien word, in one place, defined without using three more alien words to do it. Lessons link here on a term's first appearance. The list grows as lessons are written — an entry marked “introduced in …” without a link means that lesson is not written yet.

A B C D E F H J L M N O P R S T U V W X

A

aliasing
What happens when a sound contains a frequency too high for the current sample rate to represent. Rather than disappearing, it folds back down and reappears as a lower frequency that has nothing to do with the note being played — an unmusical whistle that moves the wrong way as you play up the keyboard. Introduced in g2 · Wavetables, aliasing and mipmaps
atomic
A variable that two threads may touch at the same time without the result being nonsense. Ordinary variables give no such promise: one thread can catch another mid-write and read half of an old value and half of a new one. Introduced in c6 · Handing work between threads
AVX2
A set of CPU instructions that work on eight numbers at once instead of one. This project's target CPU has it; the fallback for CPUs that do not is called SSE2, which does four at a time. See SIMD. Introduced in c2 · Eight notes at once

B

band-limiting
Deliberately removing the frequencies from a sound that are too high to be reproduced correctly, before they get a chance to cause aliasing. Introduced in g2 · Wavetables, aliasing and mipmaps
barrier
A meeting point in code: several threads each do their own work, then wait at the barrier until the slowest one arrives, and only then does anything continue. This project uses one to join its six voices back together at the end of every audio block. Introduced in c7 · Six cores, one block
block
A batch of audio frames computed in one go. Sound is not produced one sample at a time — that would be far too much overhead — but in blocks of, here, at most 128. Also called a buffer. Introduced in e1 · The audio callback
buffer
A block of memory holding audio waiting to be played or processed. Bigger buffers are safer (more time to compute) and worse (more latency). Choosing the size is the central trade-off of real-time audio. Introduced in e1 · The audio callback

C

cache line
The smallest chunk of memory a CPU actually fetches — 64 bytes on this machine. You never load one byte; you load the line it lives in. This is why two unrelated variables that happen to sit next to each other can slow each other down. See false sharing. Introduced in d3 · Storing and shaping a value
callback
A function you write but do not call. You hand it to something else — here, the sound card's driver — and it calls you, on its own schedule, when it needs something. The audio callback is the heartbeat of the whole instrument. Introduced in e1 · The audio callback
constexpr
A C++ keyword meaning "work this out while compiling, not while running". A constexpr table costs nothing at run time because it is already baked into the program. Introduced in c1 · Constants and compile-time checks
cutoff
The frequency where a filter starts doing its job — below it (for a low-pass) sound passes through, above it sound is progressively removed. Sweeping the cutoff is the single most recognisable gesture in electronic music. Introduced in h1 · What a filter is

D

denormal
A floating-point number so close to zero that the CPU has to use a slow, special representation for it. Audio produces them constantly — every fading reverb tail decays into denormal territory — and on some processors they cost a hundred times more than ordinary arithmetic. Introduced in c5 · Tiny numbers, big stalls
DSP
Digital Signal Processing. The umbrella term for doing arithmetic on sound: filtering, distorting, delaying, analysing. Introduced in a1 · What we are actually building

E

envelope
A shape over time that a note follows — typically how it swells when struck and how it fades when released. The classic one is ADSR (attack, decay, sustain, release); this project uses a six-stage version. Introduced in h3 · Things that move by themselves
Euclidean rhythm
A rhythm made by spreading a number of hits as evenly as possible over a number of steps — 5 hits in 8 steps, say. The algorithm that does it is Euclid's, and it happens to produce a startling number of the world's traditional rhythms. Introduced in k2 · Euclidean rhythms

F

false sharing
Two threads writing to two different variables, and slowing each other down anyway, because those variables share a cache line. The fix is to pad them apart. Introduced in d3 · Storing and shaping a value
FFT
Fast Fourier Transform: the algorithm that takes a slice of sound and tells you which frequencies it is made of. The reverse turns a list of frequencies back into sound. Introduced in g1 · What a spectrum is
filter
Anything that makes a sound duller, brighter or more nasal by turning some frequencies down and leaving others alone. See cutoff and resonance. Introduced in h1 · What a filter is
frame
One instant of sound: one number per channel. At 48 kHz there are 48,000 frames per second. Casually called a "sample", though strictly a sample is one number and a stereo frame contains two. Introduced in e1 · The audio callback
FX
Effects. Anything that processes sound after it has been generated — distortion, delay, reverb, chorus. Each voice here has twelve slots and any effect can go in any slot. Introduced in a1 · What we are actually building

H

harmonic
One of the simple pure tones a real sound is built from. A note at 100 Hz typically also contains energy at 200, 300, 400 Hz and so on; the recipe of harmonics is what makes a violin sound unlike a flute. Introduced in g1 · What a spectrum is
header
A C++ file, usually ending .h, containing declarations that other files paste into themselves with #include. Crucially, a header that nothing includes is never compiled at all. Introduced in c1 · Constants and compile-time checks

J

JUCE
The C++ framework this instrument is built on. It supplies the window, the drawing, the sound-card plumbing and the MIDI handling, so the project can spend its effort on the synth itself rather than on talking to Windows. Introduced in b1 · From text to a running program

L

lane
One of the slots in a SIMD register. This project puts one note in each lane, so eight notes are computed by the same instruction at the same time. Introduced in c2 · Eight notes at once
latency
The delay between something happening and you hearing it. Below roughly 10 ms it feels instant; above that an instrument starts to feel unresponsive. Introduced in e1 · The audio callback
LFO
Low Frequency Oscillator. A wave too slow to hear as a pitch, used instead as a control — wobbling a filter, panning a sound side to side, adding vibrato. Introduced in h3 · Things that move by themselves
lock-free
Code that lets threads share data without any of them ever being made to wait. It matters here because the audio thread cannot afford to wait for anything, ever. Introduced in c6 · Handing work between threads

M

mask
A set of yes/no flags, one per lane, used to apply a decision to some lanes and not others — the SIMD replacement for if. Introduced in c4 · Code without branches
memory ordering
The rules describing what one thread is guaranteed to see of another thread's writes, and in what order. Modern CPUs and compilers reorder work aggressively, so without these rules "I wrote A then B" does not mean anyone else observes A then B. Introduced in c6 · Handing work between threads
MIDI
The decades-old messaging standard musical hardware speaks. Here it carries only knob movements from the custom controller — no notes, because the sequencer plays everything. Introduced in l3 · Twelve real encoders
mipmap
Borrowed from 3D graphics: keeping several progressively blurrier copies of something and picking the right one for the situation. Here the copies are versions of a waveform with progressively fewer harmonics, used to avoid aliasing on high notes. Introduced in g2 · Wavetables, aliasing and mipmaps
modulation
Letting one thing control another automatically — an envelope opening a filter, an LFO wobbling a pitch. Modulation is what separates a synthesizer from an organ. Introduced in h3 · Things that move by themselves
modulation matrix
A grid for wiring any modulation source to any destination, with a depth for each connection — the software version of a patch bay. Introduced in j1 · The matrix
mutation testing
Deliberately introducing a bug into working code to check that the test suite notices. A test that still passes was never testing anything. Introduced in c3 · Three engines behind one name

N

Nyquist frequency
Half the sample rate — the highest frequency a digital system can represent. At 48 kHz that is 24 kHz. Anything above it comes back as aliasing. Introduced in g2 · Wavetables, aliasing and mipmaps

O

oracle test
A test that checks a fast, clever implementation against a slow, obvious one that is easy to believe. The slow version is the oracle; if they ever disagree, the clever one is wrong. Introduced in c3 · Three engines behind one name
oscillator
The part that actually makes a tone — the vibrating thing. Everything else in a synthesizer shapes what the oscillators produce. Introduced in a1 · What we are actually building

P

polyphony
How many notes can sound at once. Each of this instrument's six synths is polyphonic, handling up to 16 simultaneous notes. Introduced in a1 · What we are actually building

R

RAII
Resource Acquisition Is Initialisation — an ugly name for a beautiful idea. Tie cleanup to a variable going out of scope, and the cleanup can never be forgotten, not even when something goes wrong halfway through. Introduced in c5 · Tiny numbers, big stalls
RCU
Read-Copy-Update. Instead of editing a shared structure while others read it, you build a complete new one off to the side and swap the pointer in one indivisible step. Readers never wait and never see a half-finished state. Introduced in c6 · Handing work between threads
real-time
Not "fast" — on time. A real-time deadline that is missed is a failure even if the answer was correct, because in audio a late result is a click you can hear. Introduced in e1 · The audio callback
resonance
A filter control that emphasises the frequencies right at the cutoff, giving the characteristic vocal or squelchy quality. Turned high enough, a filter will ring on its own. Introduced in h1 · What a filter is

S

sample rate
How many times a second the sound is measured. CDs use 44,100; this project defaults to 48,000. It sets the ceiling on what frequencies can exist — see Nyquist. Introduced in e1 · The audio callback
sequencer
The part that decides which notes play when. Here it is the only source of notes: there is no keyboard input at all, by deliberate decision. Introduced in a1 · What we are actually building
SIMD
Single Instruction, Multiple Data. One CPU instruction that does the same arithmetic to several numbers simultaneously. Eight at a time here — see AVX2 and lane. Introduced in c2 · Eight notes at once
static_assert
A check the compiler performs. If it fails, the program does not build — which is far better than a program that builds and then misbehaves at 3am. Introduced in c1 · Constants and compile-time checks

T

translation unit
One .cpp file plus everything it includes — the actual unit the compiler chews on. C++ compiles these one at a time and independently, which explains a great deal about how the language behaves. Introduced in c1 · Constants and compile-time checks

U

unison
Playing several slightly detuned copies of the same note at once, so they drift in and out of phase with each other. It is why one synth patch can sound like a whole string section. Introduced in f2 · Glide, unison, and playing dynamics

V

voice
A word doing two jobs, unfortunately. In most synthesizers a voice is one sounding note. In this project a "voice" is one of the six complete independent synthesizers — each of which is itself polyphonic. This site says "voice" for the big one and "note" for the small one. Introduced in a1 · What we are actually building

W

wavetable
A stored collection of waveform shapes that an oscillator plays through and can sweep between, so the timbre morphs continuously rather than being fixed. Introduced in g2 · Wavetables, aliasing and mipmaps

X

xrun
The audio equivalent of missing a deadline: the sound card asked for the next block and it was not ready. You hear it as a click, a pop or a stutter. Counting xruns is how you know whether the instrument is really keeping up. Introduced in e1 · The audio callback
