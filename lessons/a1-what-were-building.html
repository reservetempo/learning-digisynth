Part A · Orientation

What we are actually building

Built   PLAN · Context, Decisions already made

In one sentence

One program that is six complete synthesizers running at the same time, played by a rhythm generator built into it, controlled by twelve knobs that spin forever, and drawn on a tablet screwed to the desk.

Why it exists

There is no shortage of software synthesizers. You can buy one for the price of a pizza, load it into a music program, and stack five more on top. So the first honest question about this project is why it should exist at all.

The answer is in one line of the plan: “as many effect combinations and control as desired.” That is not a feature request, it is a complaint. Stacking six software synths inside a host program means six windows, one mouse, and a machine that starts stuttering long before you have finished patching. Everything is a menu. Nothing is a knob. And the moment you want voice three to modulate voice five, you discover that the plugin format never really intended those two things to know about each other.

So this is not a plugin. It is an instrument: a single program that owns the whole machine, presents itself on a dedicated screen, and is played with your hands rather than a pointer. That decision is the reason for almost every strange choice in the rest of this site.

The shape of the thing

Start with the word that will confuse you first. In most synthesizers a voice means one sounding note — “16-voice polyphony” means sixteen notes at once. In this project a voice means one of six complete, independent synthesizers, each of which is itself polyphonic and handles up to sixteen notes of its own. Six voices, sixteen notes each. This site says voice for the big thing and note for the small thing, always.

Inside one of those six voices there is:

  • three wavetable oscillators and a sampler — the parts that actually make a tone;
  • two filters — the parts that make it dull, bright, or vocal;
  • eighteen things that move by themselves: six envelopes, eight LFOs, four random generators — plus four macro knobs;
  • twelve effect slots, reorderable, where any of the eleven effect types can go in any slot;
  • a six-row modulation matrix for wiring any of those movers to any of the several hundred controls.

Then there is a seventh section that is not a synthesizer at all. It holds the sequencer — the thing that plays notes, six independent rhythm tracks, one per voice — and a second modulation matrix whose reach crosses voice boundaries, so an envelope in voice one can open a filter in voice four.

There is no keyboard input. None. The sequencer is the only source of notes, and that was decided deliberately rather than deferred. Nor are there presets: the plan explicitly puts them out of scope, and only asks that the state stay simple enough to save later if that changes.

The number that decided everything

Sound leaves a computer in batches. The sound card asks for the next block of audio, you fill it, it asks again. This project renders at most 128 frames per block at a sample rate of 48,000 frames per second. Divide one by the other and you get the only deadline that matters:

128 ÷ 48,000 = 2.67 milliseconds, 375 times every second

In 2.67 milliseconds, six synthesizers must each render up to sixteen notes through three oscillators, two filters, twelve effects and a modulation matrix — and then all of it has to be mixed and handed back. Miss the deadline once and you do not get a slightly late result, you get an audible click. This is what real-time means: not fast, but on time, every single time, forever.

That budget was computed before a line of code was written, and it picked the architecture by itself. Two ideas fall straight out of it:

  • Spread the six voices across six CPU cores. The target machine has six fast cores. There are six voices. Each gets one, and because they share nothing while they run, they never have to wait for each other.
  • Compute eight notes at a time within a voice. Modern processors have instructions that do the same arithmetic to eight numbers simultaneously — see SIMD. Put one note in each lane and sixteen notes cost two operations instead of sixteen. Part C is entirely about making that work.

Cross-voice modulation would normally ruin the first idea, because voice four would have to ask voice one what it is doing. The plan sidesteps it: all cross-voice values are worked out before the six voices start, and are read-only for the rest of the block. The voices stay independent, and no locking is needed anywhere.

kControlBlock = 32 is the one that is not obvious. Modulation — an envelope opening a filter, an LFO wobbling a pitch — does not need to be recalculated 48,000 times a second, because nothing you can hear moves that fast. So it is worked out once every 32 frames and smoothly interpolated in between. That is a 32× saving on a large part of the arithmetic, bought for no audible cost, and it is a good example of the kind of trade this project makes constantly.

The architecture idea · let the constraint choose the design

The parallelism here was not a clever optimisation added when things got slow. It was chosen on day one, from a number — 2.67 ms — computed before anything existed. Because it came first, everything else could be shaped to fit it: the voices share nothing, cross-voice modulation is resolved early, modulation runs at a coarser rate, and effects are allocated on a different thread entirely.

Optimising later would not have produced this design. It would have produced the obvious one with locks bolted on, and locks are exactly what a 2.67 ms deadline cannot survive. When a project has one dominant constraint, find it early and let it do the arguing.

C++ · constexpr, and why not just write 6

constexpr int kNumVoices = 6; declares a value the compiler works out while building, not while running. By the time the program starts, every use of kNumVoices has already been replaced by a literal 6 — so the name costs nothing at all.

The alternative that older C++ code uses is #define kNumVoices 6, a text substitution performed before the compiler even looks at the code. It works, and it is worse in every way that matters: a macro has no type, obeys no scope, and cannot be seen by a debugger, so a mistake shows up as a baffling error somewhere else entirely. constexpr gives you the same zero cost with a real, typed, scoped name.

The header also has a second trick — static_assert, which makes the compiler check the relationships between these numbers and refuse to build if they drift apart. That gets its own lesson in c1.

Check yourself

Why not just run six copies of the program, one per synthesizer?

Three reasons. They would each want the sound card. Their sequencers would drift apart, because nothing would keep them sample-accurate with each other. And the cross-voice modulation matrix — voice one's envelope opening voice four's filter — has no way to exist across process boundaries. One process, six cores, shared clock.

What is the deadline, and what happens when it is missed?

2.67 milliseconds, 375 times a second: 128 frames at 48 kHz. Miss it and the sound card has nothing to play, which you hear as a click or a stutter — an xrun. A late answer is a wrong answer.

Why is modulation only recalculated every 32 frames?

Because nothing audible moves that fast. An envelope or LFO recalculated 1,500 times a second and smoothly interpolated in between is indistinguishable from one recalculated 48,000 times, and costs a thirty-second as much. The saved budget goes into effects and notes instead.
